Why did this happen?

    What is actually happening

    Waste oil is made of triglycerides. Think of each one as a small glycerol backbone with three long fatty acid chains attached to it. When you mix the oil with an alcohol and a catalyst, the fatty acid chains let go of the glycerol and attach to the alcohol instead. That gives you biodiesel. The leftover glycerol drops out on its own and settles at the bottom.

    Real-world note. Biodiesel is commonly used in blends such as B5 and B20. Compatibility depends on the fuel specification and engine manufacturer guidance.

    What each slider actually does

    Temperature. Increasing temperature can speed the reaction up to a suitable operating range. Excessive temperature can reduce effective alcohol availability and introduce other practical problems.

    Alcohol to oil ratio. This reaction can go backwards as well as forwards, so extra alcohol pushes it towards making more biodiesel. After about 6 parts alcohol to 1 part oil, adding more stops helping much, it just means more alcohol to separate out afterwards.

    Catalyst amount. Too little and the reaction barely gets going. Too much causes a second, unwanted reaction, see below.

    Reaction time. The reaction needs time to reach its natural limit. Cut it short and you stop before the oil has finished reacting.

    The reaction you do not want: soap

    If there is a lot of free fatty acid in the oil, or too much catalyst, the catalyst reacts directly with those acids instead of doing its normal job. This makes soap. Soap eats into your catalyst, wastes oil, and makes it much harder to get a clean split between the biodiesel layer and the glycerol layer.

    Did you know? Real biodiesel producers sometimes run an extra step first, called esterification, when the waste oil is especially acidic. It turns those free fatty acids into more biodiesel before the main reaction starts, instead of losing them to soap. This simulator does not model that step, but it is worth reading about if you want to go further.

    Feedstock quality. This is why my olive oil trial and my sunflower oil trial gave different results even under similar conditions. A cleaner oil with less free fatty acid and less water tends to give a higher, cleaner yield.

    Research question

    How can an interactive, calibrated simulation be used to show and test how feedstock choice and process conditions affect biodiesel yield from waste cooking oils, compared against real transesterification trials?

    Objectives

    Research the chemistry behind transesterification and work out which variables matter most. Build a simple mathematical model of conversion, yield and purity. Calibrate that model against real lab results. Build an interactive website around it. Test whether the model's trends make sense, and check them against further trials where possible. Evaluate its limitations honestly.

    How the model works

    Temperature, alcohol ratio, catalyst amount and time each get turned into a simple number between 0 and 1, describing how favourable that condition is. Multiplying those together gives a general trend for how far the reaction goes. The olive oil and sunflower oil feedstocks are then scaled so the model lands exactly on my measured yields, 84.9 percent and 70.3 percent, under a typical set of trial conditions. The other feedstocks use estimated numbers instead of measured ones, and the site says so wherever they are used.

    Limitations

    The model is only calibrated against two real numbers, one per tested feedstock, so it can match those exactly, but the shape of the curve between and beyond them is an assumption based on general knowledge of the reaction, not my own repeated measurements. Purity is an estimate too, not calibrated to any measured purity result. The temperature, catalyst amount and time used as the typical reference conditions are assumed rather than taken from one specific logged trial.

    Further work

    Running a few real trials that change only one condition at a time, temperature for example, would let you check the model's curve shape against real data instead of an assumption. Measuring purity directly, by titration or gas chromatography if you have access to it, would let that reading be calibrated too.
